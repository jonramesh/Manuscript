Brand States: Postmodern Power, Democratic Pluralism, and Design

by Metahaven

I N T H E N N E W E U R O P E

A grey morning in a small Baltic capital. Two dozen people are gathered in a borrowed classroom to decide how their country ought to present itself to everyone who does not live in it. Some of them work for ministries, some for agencies, one or two for nobody in particular. The current slogan is the first thing to go: nobody defends it, and nobody can say precisely what is wrong with it either, beyond the fact that it sounds like it was written for a country that does not exist. The line in question is “ Welcome to Estonia.” Everybody in the room agrees that a sentence cannot carry a nation, and everybody in the room spends the rest of the morning arguing about the sentence. This is the ordinary condition of place branding: a discussion that begins with a wordmark and ends, without anyone quite noticing the transition, with a proposal about how citizens should behave in public.

The meeting was open to the public, which did not make it democratic. Everyone present had been invited for their proximity to the subject rather than for any capacity to speak on behalf of the people whose image was being discussed. A network of specialists is not a constituency, and the difference matters more the further the conversation drifts from graphics and toward governance.

Branding has long since stopped meaning the commercial trademark alone. That expansion has made it easier to condemn and harder to describe. The wider its remit, the more obviously it operates as a form of authority; and the more obviously it operates as authority, the less precisely anyone can say where its jurisdiction ends. A place brand sits at the far end of this drift, a trademark for somewhere people actually live.

What a place brand really amounts to is a first impression, held by people who will mostly never visit. It is the two or three thoughts that arrive unbidden at the mention of a name. Moving those thoughts in a more favourable direction may call for a better logo, or a worse one, but it may equally call for changes in policy that alter the lives of the people being represented. This is what makes the practice difficult to argue about honestly: it is a visual discipline and a mode of government at the same time, and its practitioners are rarely asked to say which one they are doing.

Two political concepts do most of the work in this field. The first is widely recognised and openly discussed. The second is rarely named, though it describes the situation more accurately, and it is the one that makes the whole arrangement difficult to escape. Neither has yet produced a critical or counter-hegemonic practice of state branding, which is the absence this essay is concerned with.
